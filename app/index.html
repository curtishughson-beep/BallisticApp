About these numbers

Conditions are fixed at standard sea-level air — the same basis every manufacturer uses to print the numbers on the box, so this chart matches your ammo’s published data. That is a deliberate simplification and it costs you almost nothing: at Stonewall’s elevation on a 5 °C October morning, air density shifts the 500 yd impact by less than a tenth of an inch. Cold powder is the only ambient effect that shows up at all, and it is about 1 in at 500 yd near freezing, 3 in at −15 °C.

Muzzle velocity is the number that actually matters. Every load here uses the maker’s published figure from a 24 in test barrel, and real rifles routinely run 50–100 fps off it. At 500 yd, 50 fps is worth about 1.5 in of impact — an order of magnitude more than every weather effect combined. If you ever chronograph this rifle, tell me the number and I will set it as the default for your load — it is worth more than every other input here combined.

The two cartridges are not interchangeable at distance. With a 200 yd zero, a 162 gr ELD-X out of the 7mm asks for 18.5 in of hold at 400 yd and still carries 2006 ft·lb. A 180 gr Core-Lokt out of the .30-06 asks for 26.6 in and is down to 1342 ft·lb — under the moose energy floor before 350 yd. The .30-06 is an excellent moose cartridge, but it is a shorter-range one with standard cup-and-core loads. Pick a premium 180 gr bonded or monolithic and it stretches out considerably.

No wind is modelled here. You asked for impact and hold heights, so the chart is vertical only. For reference, a 10 mph full-value crosswind pushes a 162 gr ELD-X about 4.5 in at 300 yd and 13 in at 500 — worth knowing on open Manitoba country, and I can add a wind slider back any time.

High-BC bullets may read slightly optimistic past 300 yd. Loads with plain cup-and-core bullets match their published tables here to within about 18 fps at 500 yd. The premium high-BC bullets — ELD-X, Terminal Ascent, Elite Hunter, AccuBond Long Range — come out 50–140 fps faster than the maker's own table, and the gap scales with the advertised ballistic coefficient rather than with anything in the solver. That is the well-known tendency for advertised BCs on hunting bullets to run ahead of measured ones. Practically it means a couple of inches less holdover than reality at 400–500 yd on those loads, and nothing you would notice inside 300.

Verify on paper. This is a point-mass solver using standard G1 drag functions, validated against published factory tables to within 2–18 fps of retained velocity at 500 yd. It does not model spin drift or uphill/downhill shot angle. Confirm the hold at the ranges you actually intend to shoot before you rely on it on an animal.
